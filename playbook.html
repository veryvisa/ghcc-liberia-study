The Case Day Playbook

A visual guide to understanding newborn health in Liberia, thinking like a global health team, and presenting your own ideas with confidence.

How to use this playbook

  1. Read the official background first. This playbook does not replace it; it helps you see its shape.
  2. Tonight (20–30 min): Parts 1–3 (the day, the big picture, the diagrams). Skim the rest.
  3. Tomorrow: use Parts 4–8 as tools while your team works. Use Part 9 if anything goes wrong.
  4. Anything marked ? is a question to discuss, not an answer to copy.

Fair play: your ideas, your team

This playbook teaches how to think, not what to answer. It has no ready-made solution to the case. The practice example uses a made-up place on purpose.

Your solution should come from your team, on the day, in response to the real prompt.

Check the rules at registration: are printed notes, internet, or AI tools allowed during the work time? If unsure, ask an organizer. Asking is always fine.

Liberia in context: geography & history

The place and the past behind the numbers

Liberia at a glance

Areaabout 111,000 km², roughly the size of the island of Newfoundland
Peopleabout 5.2 million (2022 census); about a third live in and around Monrovia
Counties15; Maryland County (capital Harper) is in the far southeast, on the border with Côte d'Ivoire
Coastabout 580 km of Atlantic coast; 60% of people live within 50 km of it
Landcoastal lowlands → dense rainforest (Upper Guinea forest) → northern highlands
RainMonrovia is one of the world's rainiest capitals (over 4 m a year); rainy season May–Oct
LanguagesEnglish is official; Liberian English is widely spoken; many Indigenous languages (about 16 ethnic groups)
Faithmostly Christian, with a sizeable Muslim minority and traditional beliefs
MoneyLiberian dollar and U.S. dollar both used
Economyfarming employs ~70%; iron ore, gold, diamonds, rubber, timber exported
? Geography questions: Maryland County is about as far from Monrovia as you can get. What does that mean for staff, supplies and referrals? How does a coast-to-forest-to-hills landscape change how people travel in the rainy season?

History timeline (with the health link)

Before 1800s
Indigenous peoples such as the Kpelle, Bassa, Gio, Kru, Grebo, Mano, Vai and Gola live here for centuries. European traders from the 1400s call it the Pepper or Grain Coast.↳ Communities, chiefs and traditional midwives are older than the state.
1816–1822
The American Colonization Society forms in the U.S. (1816), pressures local peoples at Cape Mesurado to give up land (1821), and the first freed African Americans arrive (1822). About 16,000 settle before the U.S. Civil War.↳ The reading: foreign forces shaped Liberia from the start.
1824 · 1847
The colony is named Liberia ("land of the free"); the capital Monrovia is named after U.S. President James Monroe. In 1847 Liberia declares independence: Africa's first republic.
1847–1980
A small settler-descended elite (Americo-Liberians) holds power for over a century, while the Indigenous majority is largely shut out of government, schooling and wealth.↳ Roots of today's rural–urban and education gaps.
1926 · 1930
Firestone leases about one million acres for rubber. In 1930 a League of Nations inquiry finds forced labour; the president resigns.↳ Resource deals that send wealth abroad (the reading's theme).
1944–1971
President Tubman's "Open Door" policy invites foreign companies; iron ore and rubber boom, but benefits stay narrow.
1980
Samuel Doe seizes power in a military coup; years of repression follow.
1989–2003
Two civil wars (1989–97, 1999–2003) under warlords including Charles Taylor. An estimated 250,000 people die; most health facilities are destroyed; timber and diamonds pay for fighting.↳ 242 of 293 public facilities lost; trained staff flee.
2003
Liberian women's peace movement (led by Leymah Gbowee and others) pushes leaders to sign the Accra peace agreement. A UN peacekeeping mission stays until 2018.↳ Women organising changed national history.
2006–2018
Ellen Johnson Sirleaf becomes Africa's first elected female head of state (Nobel Peace Prize 2011, shared with Gbowee and Yemen's Tawakkol Karman). Rebuilding begins.
2014–2016
Ebola: nearly 5,000 deaths in Liberia; 189 health workers die; people avoid clinics.↳ First pregnancy visits −43%, facility births −38%.
2016–2024
National Community Health Assistant Program (2016). Presidents George Weah (2018) and Joseph Boakai (2024). COVID-19 (2020) cuts routine care again.
2025–2026
The U.S. cuts foreign aid: Liberia gets 35% less U.S. aid and loses ~US$434M of planned projects. New Community Health Program Policy 2025–2035. Newborn deaths: 33 per 1,000 (2024).↳ Where your case begins.

Facts not in the official reading (area, population, peoples, presidents, Firestone, peace movement, Ebola deaths, rainfall) come from standard references such as the national census and encyclopaedias. They are rounded: double-check before quoting a number in your pitch.

? History questions: How might a century of exclusion shape whether rural families trust government clinics? Why might outside "help" be received carefully? What does the women's peace movement suggest about who can lead change in communities?

In a pitch, two or three well-chosen numbers beat ten. Pick the ones that prove your focus.

The four "Canada connection" boxes

  • p.3 A flooded gravel road in rural Canada, for six months a year
  • p.6 COVID strained Canada too; Liberia had far less room to absorb it
  • p.7 Canadians don't pay at the point of care; Liberian families pay in the emergency
  • p.10 Canada has incubators and newborn units; Liberia lacks working incubators and is far from hospitals

The organizers want you to compare thoughtfully: what Canada does that could transfer cheaply, and what depends on money Liberia doesn't have.

What the reading quietly emphasises

  • It ends on community health workers and TTMs, with their needs.
  • It returns to gender in three places: profile, distal causes and workforce.
  • It names PIH's 5 S's and PIH's own Maryland County work.
  • It stresses sustainability: donor money and the 2025 cut.

2D

The core question: history, neighbours, why Liberia?

What the long-term data show (UN IGME estimates, 1990–2024)

Is Liberia the worst? No: 7th of 200 countries (2024)

#rank among 200 countries. Sierra Leone was worse than Liberia in 2010 (38.8 vs 34.8) and is now better (28.3 vs 32.9).

The access paradox

Facility births more than doubled (37% → 80%). Newborn deaths fell only about 10% over the same years.

Newborns are now the hardest part of child survival in Liberia

Deaths of children under five fell by about two-thirds since 1990. Newborn deaths fell by less than half, so newborns now make up 38% of all child deaths, up from 23%.

? So why did the organizers choose Liberia?

  • Not because it is the worst. Six countries are worse, including Nigeria and South Sudan.
  • Because its progress stalled. 1990–2010: down 43%. 2010–2024: down 5%.
  • Because more access didn't bring survival. Mothers came to facilities; babies kept dying.
  • Because neighbours with similar pasts did better. Sierra Leone also lived through civil war and Ebola, yet kept improving.
  • Because PIH works there, in Maryland County, and the 2025 aid cut makes this a live test.

? Questions the data raise

  • What changed around 2010 that made progress slow down?
  • Why would more facility births not mean fewer newborn deaths? Think about the first 24 hours and Delay 3.
  • What did Sierra Leone, Guinea and Côte d'Ivoire do in the same years? One lead to look into: in 2010 Sierra Leone made care free for pregnant women and young children.
  • Why did deaths of older children fall faster than deaths of newborns?
  • Will the 2025 aid cut push the line back up?
One way to state the core question

The puzzle is not only "why do newborns die in Liberia?" It is why survival stopped improving after 2010, even as far more mothers reached health facilities, and what can restart progress now that outside money is shrinking. Does your team agree? Rewrite it in your own words once you see the real prompt.

Sources: UN Inter-agency Group for Child Mortality Estimation (UN IGME), estimates via UNICEF Data, downloaded 13 Nov 2026 (2024 value 32.9 matches the reading's 33). Facility births: Liberia Demographic and Health Surveys 2007, 2013 and 2019–20. Estimates are modelled and revised each year; quote them as "about".

Thinking tools & templates

Methods consultants and global health teams use; fill them with your own ideas

PIH's 5 S's: a checklist for any health solution

Partners In Health (the organizer) says a health system needs all five. If one is missing, care breaks down, which makes the five a great way to test your idea.

Logic model: from money to lives saved

Impact / effort grid

Put each idea on a sticky note or in a shared doc, then place it. Prefer the top half.

More quick tools

  • 5 Whys. Ask "why?" five times. Baby died → born too small → mother undernourished → no money for food → she doesn't control income → …
  • Stand in their shoes. Retell the case from the mother, the mother-in-law, a CHA, a nurse, the health minister. What does each need?
  • Pre-mortem. "It's 2030 and our plan failed. Why?" List reasons, then fix the top two.
  • Who pays · who runs · who measures. If you can't answer all three, the plan isn't finished.
  • Dry season vs rainy season. Test every idea in both.

Practice case: a worked example

A made-up place and a different disease, so you can see the method without seeing an answer

Practice prompt (fictional)

"Kelo is a rural district where many children under five die of malaria. Most families live 2–3 hours' walk from the clinic, and the river floods for four months a year. You advise the district health office, with a modest budget for three years. Propose one intervention to reduce child malaria deaths."

1 · Unpack the prompt

Question: one intervention. Role: advisers to the district. Limits: modest budget, 3 years, floods. Outcome: fewer child deaths.

2 · Find the root

Problem tree: deaths ← treatment comes too late ← clinic far, floods, cost, fever seen as "normal". Biggest delay here: reach.

3 · Go wide

Ideas: bed nets, mobile clinic, village volunteers who test and treat, boat ambulance, free care, radio messages, spraying…

4 · Choose

Score with the matrix. "Village volunteers test and treat at home" scores high on reach, cost and lasting. Big hospital scores low.

5 · Build it with the 5 S's

Staff: 20 volunteers. Stuff: test kits, medicine. Space: their homes. Systems: monthly restock, referral for severe cases. Social: small stipend, chiefs' support.

6 · Prove and protect it

Logic model + indicators (% fevers treated within 24 h). Risks: stock-outs in floods → stock up before rains.

Building your solution

What judges in global health case competitions usually look for

Signs of a strong solution

  • Answers the exact prompt: the question, the role, the limits.
  • One clear focus, explained deeply, beats many shallow ideas.
  • Rooted in the reading: uses its numbers, places and people.
  • Feasible here: works with mud roads, little power, few staff.
  • Sustainable: who keeps it going when outside money stops?
  • Equitable: reaches the poorest, most remote, youngest mothers.
  • Community-led: designed with Liberians, building on what exists.
  • Measurable: clear indicators and targets.
  • Honest about risks, with a plan for each.

Common traps (teams fall in every year)

  • Deciding the solution before reading the prompt.
  • A list of ten ideas and no depth.
  • Tech that needs internet, power or money that isn't there.
  • "Build a big new hospital" with no staff or budget plan.
  • "Donors will pay" right after donors cut funding.
  • Ignoring the rainy season or the first 24 hours.
  • Blaming "culture" instead of understanding reasons.
  • Outsiders "saving" Liberia; the reading's history section is a hint.
  • Mixing up mother deaths and newborn deaths.

Presenting & slides

A clear story beats a crowded slide deck

Slide rules

  • Titles that say the point: "Most babies die on day one", not "Statistics".
  • ≤ 20 words per slide; font ≥ 24 pt.
  • One number per slide, big.
  • Cite the reading: "(LDHS 2019–20)".
  • Same colours and fonts throughout.
  • Export a PDF copy as a backup.

Speaking

  • Open with a person or a striking fact, not "Hi, we're going to talk about…"
  • Everyone speaks; hand off by name: "Now Sam will show how it works."
  • Speak slower than feels natural; pause after key numbers.
  • Look at the judges, not the screen.
  • End by returning to your opening.

Rehearsal

  • Run it twice with a timer, out loud.
  • Aim 10–15% under the limit: real talks run long.
  • Agree what to cut if time runs short.
  • Practise transitions between speakers.
  • One teammate plays a tough judge.
A useful storytelling trick: invent a mother (give her a name your team chooses) and follow her from pregnancy to her baby's first week. Show where things go wrong today and where your plan catches her. Judges remember people more than percentages.

Judges' questions

Q&A is often where close teams are separated. Prepare the method, not scripts.

The seven kinds of questions judges usually ask (prepare one sentence for each)

TypeExample questionWhat they're really testing
Cost"How much would this cost, and who pays after year 3?"Sustainability after aid cuts
Feasibility"How does this work in the rainy season?"Did you use the context?
Acceptance"Why would families or elders agree to this?"Understanding of gender, power, trust
Evidence"What makes you think this works?"Use of data and research
Measurement"How would you know it's working after one year?"Indicators and targets
Risk"What's the biggest thing that could go wrong?"Honesty and planning
Priorities"If you could only fund one part, which?"Clear thinking about trade-offs

Good habits

  • Decide beforehand who answers which type.
  • Keep answers under 30 seconds.
  • It's fine to add to a teammate's answer: "To build on that…"
  • Write the question down if it's long.
  • Thank the judge; stay friendly even if the question feels hard.

If you don't know

"That's a great question. We didn't have time to work that out today. Our first step would be to ask the county health team and the communities themselves."

Honest and calm beats making up numbers. Judges respect teams that know the limits of their plan.

9

When things go wrong

Every team hits a bump. Here is what to do.

SituationWhat to do
The prompt is different from what we expectedGood: everyone is in the same position. Re-read the prompt together; underline the question. Keep only the background knowledge that helps answer it.
The team can't agreeSet a time limit (5 min), score ideas with the matrix, and vote. Combine ideas if possible. "Disagree and commit": once decided, everyone backs it.
One person dominates / someone is quietUse silent writing: everyone writes ideas for 5 minutes, then reads them aloud in turn. Give each person an owned part.
A teammate is missing or lateRedistribute roles early. Brief them in 2 minutes when they arrive and hand them a clear task.
We're running out of timeStop adding ideas. Finish the core slides (problem, solution, how, measure) first; polish last. A simple finished deck beats a beautiful half-deck.
Laptop dies / no Wi-Fi / file lostSave often, in two places (cloud + a second laptop or USB). Keep one offline copy and a PDF. Worst case: present from notes or a whiteboard; judges care about ideas.
No projector in the small roomPresent from a laptop turned toward the judges, or speak without slides. Practise one run without looking at slides.
Mind goes blank while presentingPause, breathe, look at your slide title, say its main point in one sentence. A teammate can step in: "Let me add to that."
Going over timeSkip to your solution and your close; never skip the ending. Watch for the timekeeper's signals.
A judge interrupts or challenges hardThat shows interest. Thank them, answer briefly, and continue from where you were.
The topic feels upsettingThe reading warns that some topics are heavy. It's okay to step outside for a few minutes, get water, or tell an organizer or a teammate. Caring is part of global health.
Feeling overwhelmed or nervousNormal for everyone, even judges. Slow breath in for 4, out for 6, three times. Focus on the next small task only.
We don't advance to the finalMost teams don't. Watch the finals and note three things the finalists did well. That's the real prize of a first competition.
We do advance to the finalYou may have only minutes. Fix the one weakness judges raised, re-check timing, and present the same story to a bigger room.

Question bank: every way this case could be asked

Use it to practise thinking, not to predict the answer

The prompt generator: most case prompts mix these four ingredients

Practice: pick one item from each column and ask, "How would our thinking change?" Tomorrow, find all four ingredients in the real prompt.

Possible competition prompts to practise with (invented for practice; the real one may be different)

Role & scope
  1. You advise Liberia's Ministry of Health. Propose one intervention to reduce neonatal deaths in Maryland County within 3 years.
  2. You are PIH-Liberia. With a limited budget, which ONE of the 5 S's would you invest in first, and why?
  3. Design a plan that a county health team could run with the staff it already has.
Timing
  1. Many newborn deaths happen in the first 24 hours. Design a way to make sure skilled help is there at that moment.
  2. How can care in the first week after birth reach babies who go home the same day?
The three delays
  1. Choose the delay you think matters most for newborns and design a solution to it.
  2. Design a referral system that still works in the rainy season.
  3. Facility births are high, yet deaths stay high. Improve the quality of care (Delay 3).
Money & sustainability
  1. U.S. aid has been cut by about a third. How should Liberia protect newborn care?
  2. Make the case to a new donor: why fund newborn health in Liberia, and how will the program outlive the grant?
  3. Families pay 63% of costs themselves. How can a family avoid paying at the moment of an emergency?
People, gender & trust
  1. How can Trained Traditional Midwives and community health workers work together to save newborns?
  2. Design an approach for adolescent mothers.
  3. How could husbands, mothers-in-law and elders become allies for newborn survival?
  4. Research found a large gender imbalance among CHAs and their supervisors, with far fewer women. Why does that matter, and what would you change?
Systems & data
  1. Design a way to learn from every newborn death so the next one is prevented.
  2. How should the health system prepare so the next epidemic doesn't stop maternal and newborn care?
Canada & the world
  1. What can Canada's experience caring for remote communities teach Liberia, and what wouldn't transfer?
  2. What is the right role for outside partners, given Liberia's history?

For any prompt, first answer: What exactly is asked? Who are we? What are the limits? Which delay or cause does it point to? What would success look like?

Thinking questions for each branch of the mind map

Place & climate
  • How does a six-month rainy season change every plan?
  • What could climate change do to newborn health over the next 20 years?
  • Why might people near the coast face different risks from people inland?
History & money
  • Why can a country rich in minerals have such a poor health system?
  • What does it mean for a health system when half its budget comes from outside?
  • Is aid always good? What are its side effects?
Gender & power
  • How can a woman's power at home affect her baby's chance of survival?
  • Why might a girl's schooling be a newborn health intervention?
  • How do you respect a culture and still challenge harmful practices?
Crises
  • Why did facility births fall during Ebola even for women without Ebola?
  • What makes a health system resilient?
  • Which lessons from COVID in Canada apply here?
Health system
  • What makes a clinic a place women want to give birth?
  • Why is a working incubator about more than buying one?
  • What is the difference between access and quality?
Medical causes
  • Why are tiny babies so vulnerable to cold and infection?
  • Which causes could be prevented before birth, which at birth, which after?
  • What can be done without electricity?
Home & environment
  • What danger signs should every family know?
  • How do water, sanitation and cooking smoke affect newborns?
  • How might poverty make each of the three delays worse?
Community health
  • What does a community health worker need to do the job well?
  • Why are Traditional Midwives trusted, and how could that trust help?
  • Should community health workers be volunteers or paid staff?

Going further: questions beyond the case

  • Ethics: if money only allows one county, who decides which community goes first?
  • Measurement: is the neonatal mortality rate enough? What else should we count?
  • Comparison: Canada's newborn death rate is 3.3. Is it the same for every group in Canada?
  • Careers: midwife, epidemiologist, nurse, health economist, engineer, policy maker. Which role would each play in this case?
  • Research: what is one question about this case you would want to study?
  • Personal: what is one idea from today you could explain to a friend in one minute?

Glossary: words and acronyms you'll hear

ANC antenatal (pregnancy) care check-ups
CHA Community Health Assistant: serves homes 5 km+ from a facility
CHSS Community Health Service Supervisor
CHP Community Health Promoter: within 5 km of a facility
TTM Trained Traditional Midwife: trusted community birth helper who refers to facilities
CHW community health worker (all the above)
NMR neonatal mortality rate: deaths in days 0–28 per 1,000 live births
LBW low birth weight (< 2,500 g)
Asphyxia not enough oxygen around birth
Sepsis life-threatening reaction to infection
UHC Universal Health Coverage: care without financial hardship
Out-of-pocket money families pay directly
SDG 3 UN goal: good health and well-being
LDHS Liberia Demographic and Health Survey (2019–20)
MoH Ministry of Health
PIH-L Partners In Health Liberia (works in Maryland County)
J.J. Dossen only secondary-level hospital in the southeast offering surgery, internal medicine, obstetrics and paediatrics
USAID U.S. Agency for International Development (nearly all projects cancelled in 2025)
Proximal / distal direct causes / underlying causes
Referral sending a patient to a higher-level facility
